## Main conclusion

Under authenticated trusted configuration, correct registration, a properly generated secret scalar for the auditor, independently fresh encryption randomness, the security assumptions of the group/signature/SNARK/hash, and successful proof verification against reconstructed public inputs, the circuit expresses the following *conditional* statement: a valid participant credential, its event receipt and the encrypted registered point use the same participant secret; the encrypted point is in the intended subgroup and a matching auditor secret can recover it. The existing positive test demonstrates a sample proof and local decryption, and several sample tamperings are rejected. No mathematical counterexample was established in this review to that narrowly stated relation.

Use precise wording such as **“proof-bound encryption of a registrar-linked identity point with local auditor opening”**. An unqualified claim that third parties can independently verify the auditor's opening is not supported: the code outputs no proof of correct decryption, no authenticated identity mapping, and no enforceable opening authorization. Further, the accountability circuit is not integrated into the region gateway's report acceptance path.

## Arithmetic and relation

1. The circuit inherits the region proof and credential/event relation, then constrains `D = MiMC(101,s)`, a CA signature on `MiMC(301,D,M.x,M.y)`, and point ElGamal `R=[r]G`, `S=M+[r]P`. The inherited credential is also a CA signature on `D`; the receipt and task nullifier refer to the same witness `s`. A verifier must reconstruct the task, challenge, payload context, region root, CA and auditor public inputs from authorized sources. Under collision resistance/signature unforgeability and proof soundness, these equalities support report-to-credential-to-identity-point linkage; they do not establish that the report's measurements are factually true.

2. `accountabilityGroupCheck` checks curve membership, rejects the neutral point, computes `[q]P` with explicit fixed-bit double-and-add, and asserts the result is neutral. The checked library supplies subgroup order `q = 2736030358979909402780800718157159386076813972158567259200215660948447373041` and cofactor 8; an independent `sympy.isprime(q)` check returned `True`. Conditional on the curve parameters and the arithmetic gadget, this rejects nonidentity low-order/mixed-subgroup points for both secret identity point and public auditor point. Identity is an arbitrary group point, not an actual person identifier. The test explicitly has the CA sign a low-order identity and shows the circuit solver rejects it.

3. The local addition computes `v=d*x1*y2*x2*y1` and asserts `1+v != 0` and `1-v != 0` before gnark's unchecked divisions. Doubling asserts `a*x²+y² != 0` and `2-(a*x²+y²) != 0`; these match the gnark v0.16.4 formulas inspected. The binary multiplication initializes the neutral point, iterates from the highest constrained bit and conditionally adds the validated point. This avoids the new encryption gadget's use of the scalar-multiplication GLV hint. It is not a separate audit or security proof of the inherited EdDSA and region gadgets; those remain dependencies. Denominator conditions can also reject an exceptional input, so completeness over every theoretically valid subgroup point was not proved by one positive example.

4. Randomness is constrained to `[1,q-1]`, decomposed into `q.BitLen()` boolean bits, and used in both multiplications. `CipherR` and `CipherS` are asserted on-curve; equality to the checked arithmetic results places `R` and `S` in the correct subgroup conditional on soundness of those operations. `S` can legitimately be neutral. Native `NewAccountabilityScalar` samples uniformly from `[1,q-1]`, but the circuit cannot enforce fresh, independent randomness. In particular, `NewAccountabilityAssignment` accepts caller-supplied values.

5. Native `validateAccountabilityPoint` checks curve membership, optional neutral exclusion, and `[q]P=0`; the verifier checks the CA's compressed-key encoding by re-encoding, checks the auditor and ciphertext points, and pins a copied CA byte string and auditor value. The `RegionPolicy` object is supplied as trusted configuration. The constructor does not authenticate who selected those values: the caller must authenticate them and use the correct AccountabilityCircuit verification key. `PublicStatement` constructs inputs only; it does not itself call Groth16 verification or enforce challenge expiry, challenge consumption or duplicate suppression.

## Concrete counterexamples and boundaries

**Opening is not automatically verified or authorized.** Anyone able to call `OpenAccountabilityCiphertext` may pass a chosen well-formed ciphertext, a self-chosen auditor public point and its matching secret. The function compares the two keys but does not compare them with the auditor pinned for the report being investigated, does not receive or verify a proof, and does not check a warrant or authorization. This is misuse of an explicitly documented precondition, not evidence of a proof forgery. The integrating application must first verify the exact `(report, challenge, cipher, trusted auditor, CA, task, root)` statement and bind that verified record to the opening request. For third-party verification of an opening, add and verify a suitable proof of correct decryption or another independently verifiable mechanism; no such artifact exists now.

**ElGamal is malleable absent the bound proof.** Given `(R,S)` and any valid point `Q`, `(R,S+Q)` decrypts to `M+Q`. The original Groth16 proof rejects this modified public ciphertext in the existing test, so this is not an attack on correctly verified proof-bound reports. But `OpenAccountabilityCiphertext` alone has no ciphertext authenticity or proof check; opening tampered bytes as if they belonged to an accepted report can return a different valid point. Opening and evidence retention must therefore use the exact verified ciphertext, not an unverified replacement.

**Randomness reuse creates explicit linkage.** If the same `r` and auditor `P` are reused, the public `R` is identical; for the same registered point the entire identity ciphertext is identical even across different tasks, and for different points `S1-S2=M1-M2`. The circuit cannot distinguish deliberate reuse from a fresh sample. The assignment helper accepts reused `r`; a secure integration must independently sample and protect randomness for each submission and cannot claim unconditional cross-task unlinkability. Proof zero knowledge also does not hide public nullifier, task policy, network metadata or any such public ciphertext equality.

**The registered point is not a real identity in this prototype.** The test samples a random scalar and forms `M=[m]G`; `NewAccountabilityAssignment` has the fixture authority sign the link. There is no person/vehicle-to-point registry, ownership confirmation, uniqueness enforcement, request authorization, opening audit, real CA issuance or identity lookup. A CA that signs an incorrect `(D,M)` can misattribute the resulting credential; strong framing-resistance would require a precise registration model and further evidence. Voluntary credential sharing and stolen keys remain outside this relation. Therefore the only actual recovered object is `M`, not a person's name or vehicle identity.

**No end-to-end accountable gateway.** `RegionGateway` verifies a `RegionCircuit` public witness and corresponding key, while `AccountabilityVerifier` builds a different `AccountabilityCircuit` witness. The current code has no single acceptance path combining accountability proof verification, confidential envelope authentication, replay-safe state transitions and exact retained opening evidence. Verifier-side traceability must not be claimed for every accepted gateway report until integrated and tested. Existing gateway state is also volatile and scoped to one process.

**Cryptographic privacy and soundness remain conditional.** Point ElGamal confidentiality is usually argued under a suitable DDH-style group assumption and fresh hidden randomness; the proof's hidden witnesses rely on the actual zero-knowledge setup and implementation. `CipherR` and `CipherS` are public by design. There is no formal reduction, no established side-channel analysis, no revocation, and no basis for calling the complete system post-quantum merely because report transport uses ML-KEM; the elliptic-curve and Groth16 components use classical assumptions.

## Tests actually observed

Ran `go test -run '^TestAccountability' -count=1 -timeout=10m -v` under the current module with Go 1.26.8. Both tests passed. The AccountabilityCircuit compiled to 62,627 constraints in this run. One real Groth16 proof was constructed, checked against the pinned public statement, and locally opened to the selected point. A modified ciphertext and different pinned auditor fail verification of that fixed proof. A validly signed wrong/low-order identity link, the wrong holder's link, zero/order randomness, neutral points, altered `R`, and changed link signature fail the in-circuit solver; wrong opening key and malformed native points/scalars are also rejected. This does not mean every negative case was independently proved and verified, does not prove universal security, and does not measure field operation latency or full end-to-end acceptance.

Suggested tests before integrated claims: honest end-to-end accountability gateway; altered transcript or ciphertext after proof verification; changed public auditor/configuration; same randomness under distinct tasks; signed wrong identity under a compromised or faulty registrar; coherent ciphertexts with `r=1` and `r=q-1`; mixed-torsion input; identity-neutral `S`; concurrent duplicate acceptance; and authorization/audit behavior once an actual opening workflow exists. For each negative case distinguish native rejection, R1CS unsatisfiability, actual proof verification and outer gateway state.
