\begin{abstract}
Privacy-preserving location proofs typically certify a predicate over one hidden coordinate, but cryptographic correctness does not ensure that the underlying event is well supported when positioning is uncertain or authenticated sources can be Byzantine. We address this gap with a report-bound evidence-certification relation over private signed spacetime uncertainty boxes. Given Byzantine and honest-miscoverage budgets, a report is accepted only when the threshold-supported feasible set is nonempty and entirely contained in the authorized policy; ambiguous and inconsistent evidence remain explicit outcomes. We realize the relation with two Groth16/BN254 circuits that verify EdDSA/MiMC-signed evidence and bind the proof to the task, roster, report, threshold, challenge, and expiry. Gateway tests reject evidence tampering, substitution, stale challenges, and cross-challenge replay; an eight-copy concurrent replay accepts one copy. Across 12 calibrated synthetic conditions within the configured fault budget, the robust rule achieves 87.52\% mean decisive coverage versus 75.63\% for a fixed-margin rule. In a focus condition with the Byzantine budget respected, no wrong decisive output is observed when the theorem premise $M\le r$ holds. The smallest authenticated robust circuits require 32,474--35,248 constraints, compared with 408--612 for a weaker same-stack hidden-point circuit; subset proofs are 164 bytes with 163--255~ms median proving time on an Apple M1. The prototype is a provisioned evidence-certificate layer for vehicular crowdsensing, not an end-to-end crowdsensing platform. The results validate the finite relation and implementation, not field calibration or issuer independence; physical interpretation still depends on trusted enrollment and calibrated source coverage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Privacy-preserving verification often begins with a clean abstraction: a hidden attribute is assumed to exist, and the prover shows that it satisfies a public predicate. In location-sensitive services, for example, a user may prove that a private coordinate lies inside an authorized region without disclosing the coordinate itself~\cite{ZKLP,VehiclePolicy}. That abstraction is harder to sustain in physical sensing. Position and time estimates carry uncertainty, an authenticated source may be miscalibrated, and a compromised source may deliberately sign misleading evidence. A cryptographically correct proof over one selected point can therefore certify a statement that is formally valid yet poorly supported by the underlying observations. This motivates a stronger question: when does a collection of authenticated but uncertain evidence justify the policy claim being proved?

Vehicular crowdsensing makes this tension explicit. Vehicles contribute distributed observations to transportation and environmental sensing tasks~\cite{Suhag2023,Zheng2025BARM}, while many applications condition acceptance on where and when the reported event occurred. Revealing raw positioning evidence would undermine mobility privacy, yet reducing several uncertain observations to one convenient coordinate can hide disagreement precisely where the policy boundary matters most. At the other extreme, intersecting every source range can also be unsafe: one Byzantine source may exclude the true state while leaving a deceptively small compliant intersection. Privacy protection therefore has to be coupled with an evidence semantics that remains meaningful under uncertainty and adversarial participation.

We formulate the problem as a privacy-preserving security relation over authenticated uncertainty. Each enrolled issuer signs an axis-aligned integer box for the same report-bound reference event. With $n$ issuers, Byzantine budget $f$, honest-miscoverage budget $r$, and threshold $k=n-f-r$, the robust feasible set contains every state supported by at least $k$ signed boxes. A report is authorized only when this set is nonempty and entirely contained in the public policy. Separate reachability predicates inside and outside the policy distinguish valid, invalid, ambiguous, and inconsistent evidence instead of forcing every event into a binary decision.

The construction combines established fault-tolerant fusion ideas~\cite{Marzullo1990,BFTPoLoc,PG,CRSF} with zero-knowledge certification. The novelty is not a new SNARK primitive or a new sensor-fusion theorem. It is the security composition that turns robust uncertain evidence into a private, report-bound compliance certificate and makes the trust and calibration assumptions explicit.

The scope is deliberately narrower than an end-to-end vehicular crowdsensing system. The certificate layer assumes an authorized task and a provisioned issuer roster, and it returns a task-bound compliance decision/proof to a host application. Participant recruitment, incentives, reputation, Sybil-resistant enrollment, anonymous transport, dynamic partial participation, and network-level availability are outside the proved relation and the present prototype.

The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
 \item We define robust spacetime-compliance semantics for signed uncertainty boxes under explicit Byzantine and honest-miscoverage budgets. The four-valued decision prevents vacuous acceptance and yields a conditional soundness statement that separates cryptographic validity from physical coverage assumptions.
 \item We give two exact finite-domain realizations of the relation: lower-corner maximum-depth reachability and threshold-subset enumeration. Both circuits verify issuer signatures inside the proved relation and bind the private boxes to the public policy, roster, threshold, report context, challenge, and expiry.
 \item We implement a task-authorized gateway that reconstructs trusted public inputs, rejects roster/key inconsistencies, verifies the proof, and atomically consumes a challenge after successful verification. The resulting protocol addresses signed-evidence tampering, cross-task/report substitution, and same-challenge replay within the stated local model. This gateway is the certificate-layer interface to a host crowdsensing application rather than a replacement for its recruitment, incentive, identity-governance, or availability mechanisms.
 \item We evaluate semantics and implementation separately. The study includes 64,800 synthetic observations, matched analyses across miscoverage budgets, a 787-state UrbanNav reference-trajectory replay with simulated evidence, 300 measured Groth16 proofs across two backends, a resource-bounded compilation sweep, and a pinned same-runner reproduction of a closely related open-source ZK location baseline. These experiments validate the implemented security relation and its cost; they are not presented as field calibration of real multisource vehicular positioning.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Vehicular privacy and authenticated sensing}
Vehicular security research has developed strong mechanisms for hiding identities, protecting sensing inputs, and validating participation. AARE couples anonymous authentication with privacy-preserving reliability evaluation, while BARM adds reputation management~\cite{Cheng2023,Zheng2025BARM}; PPVDA protects verifiable sensing aggregation~\cite{Zhou2024}; BCS-LPP protects sensing locations~\cite{Shen2024}; and cooperative Sybil detection spans vehicle- and RSU-level analysis~\cite{Sultana2024}. Recent JISA work likewise studies anonymous authentication, aggregate signatures, and authenticated-data SNARKs~\cite{Imam2025JISA,Wang2025JISA,Reinhart2026ADSC}. These mechanisms secure who participates, what is disclosed, or how an authenticated computation is checked. Our focus is different: given several authenticated but uncertain observations of the same event, what should a privacy-preserving verifier be willing to certify?

\subsection{Zero-knowledge location verification}
Location privacy ranges from application-aware obfuscation~\cite{Mdee2022,Atmaca2024} to credential-based protection~\cite{Ghani2025,Zhang2025Sybil,Wang2025ZKAuth}. More directly related, ZKLP proves private geometric predicates over hidden coordinates~\cite{ZKLP}, while vehicle-policy proofs certify signed trajectories for subsidies and taxation~\cite{VehiclePolicy}. These works establish that useful location predicates can be verified without disclosing the underlying trajectory. Their proof objects, however, do not model the threshold-supported uncertainty relation considered here when multiple sources disagree, carry bounded uncertainty, or include Byzantine participants.

\subsection{Byzantine-robust evidence fusion}
Fault-tolerant interval reasoning provides the complementary perspective~\cite{Marzullo1990}. BFT-PoLoc uses Byzantine-resistant geometry for Internet-delay localization~\cite{BFTPoLoc}, whereas PG and CRSF protect Byzantine sensor fusion in distributed secure-computation settings~\cite{PG,CRSF}. Those systems address robust inference and, in some cases, stronger liveness guarantees than our fixed-roster prototype. Their primary objective is secure fusion rather than a portable zero-knowledge certificate that a threshold-supported uncertain event satisfies a public spacetime policy.

Table~\ref{tab:related-work} therefore reflects a gap between two mature lines of work: privacy-preserving policy verification usually starts from a hidden point or trajectory, while Byzantine fusion usually ends with a fused result. The framework developed next connects these ends by making the robust evidence relation itself the statement proved in zero knowledge. Relative to hidden-point systems such as ZKLP and vehicle-policy proofs, the added property is authenticated threshold-supported uncertainty under an explicit Byzantine/miscoverage budget; the cost is signature verification, larger statements, a circuit-specific setup, and fixed-roster liveness. Relative to PG/CRSF-style private fusion, the added artifact is a portable noninteractive policy certificate; the present prototype does not inherit their stronger participation or delivery guarantees. Generic secure computation could evaluate the same finite function; our contribution lies in the signed-box relation, report binding, exact circuit realizations, and explicit separation between cryptographic and physical assumptions.
\section{Proposed Framework}
The framework preserves uncertainty instead of collapsing it prematurely into a point estimate. Starting from signed regions that refer to the same event, it derives a threshold-supported feasible set, binds the resulting decision to a concrete report and challenge, and proves that decision without revealing the regions themselves. The following subsections develop these pieces in the order in which their semantics depend on one another.

\subsection{System model and assumptions}
The reference event is $z^\star=(x,y,t)$, or generally a point in
\begin{equation}
 \Omega=\prod_{a=1}^{d}[L_a,H_a]\cap\mathbb{Z}^d.
\end{equation}
A public policy is an integer box $P\subseteq\Omega$, and issuer $i$ signs an inclusive integer uncertainty box $U_i\subseteq\Omega$ under a common context $c$. Coordinates share a public frame, epoch, units, and quantization rule; the evaluated circuit configurations use unsigned 16-bit bounds. Exactness therefore refers to this finite model, not to unquantized physical space.

The host crowdsensing application supplies the authorized task, report bytes, provisioned roster, and calibration profile to this layer; the layer returns a proof-backed classification/authorization decision. It does not decide who should be recruited, how contributors are rewarded, whether independently named issuers are administratively independent, or how a dynamic active set should be formed. Those functions must be provided by the deployment that consumes the certificate.

Signatures authenticate submitted bounds but not their physical accuracy. We assume at most $f$ Byzantine issuers and at most $r$ additional honest boxes that miss $z^\star$; honest errors may be dependent. The counted unit is an enrolled authority, not merely a public key, so multiple keys or estimates under common control do not create independent trust domains~\cite{Zhang2025Sybil}. A missing roster signature aborts the protocol rather than producing an ``inconsistent'' decision.

The public statement contains $c$, $\Omega$, $P$, threshold $k$, a decision code, and ordered issuer keys. Issuer $i$ signs
\begin{equation}
 m_i=\operatorname{MiMC}(c,i,\ell_{i1},u_{i1},\ldots,\ell_{id},u_{id}).
\end{equation}
The context binds protocol/task identifiers, coordinate convention, calibration profile, domain, policy, threshold, roster, report digest, challenge nonce, and expiry. The gateway reconstructs these values from its trusted task snapshot instead of accepting a vehicle-selected public witness.

The finite model acquires a physical meaning only when every issuer maps the same real event into the common frame conservatively. That step depends on validated calibration for ranging, perception, synchronization, frame registration, and other error sources that the proof circuit cannot observe. A common-mode bias can therefore violate the coverage premise even when every signature is valid. The fixed roster creates a separate liveness tradeoff: one withholding member can halt the protocol, whereas permitting partial participation would require a newly authenticated active set and a correspondingly redefined fault/coverage budget. These choices place the present prototype in provisioned, delay-tolerant verification rather than opportunistic safety-critical V2X control.

The same conservatism is required at the continuous-to-discrete boundary. A physical uncertainty region should map to every quantized cell it intersects, while policy acceptance should require those cells to lie wholly inside the permitted region. The prototype assumes this adapter has already been applied and rejects rather than clamps out-of-range coordinates.

These assumptions deliberately separate two questions that are often conflated. Signatures and the proof system can establish which evidence was certified and under which task context; they cannot by themselves establish whether a physical sensor was well calibrated. The latter enters the soundness argument explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:security}.

\subsection{Robust spacetime compliance semantics}
The verifier should accept only when the evidence leaves no threshold-supported state outside the authorized policy. This universal requirement is stricter than selecting one convenient point and more tolerant of Byzantine outliers than intersecting every source.
For $1\leq k=n-f-r\leq n$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:feasible}
 \F=\{z\in\Omega:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind[z\in U_i]\geq k\}.
\end{equation}
Let $I=\ind[\F\cap P\neq\varnothing]$ and $O=\ind[\F\cap(\Omega\setminus P)\neq\varnothing]$. These two bits give all four outcomes in Table~\ref{tab:decisions}. The inconsistent outcome is called evidence failure in the implementation and result tables. It is a proved semantic outcome obtained only after the configured evidence has passed protocol checks; a missing signature, malformed roster, expired challenge, or failed proof instead causes protocol abort before classification. Distinguishing inconsistency avoids vacuous acceptance: the empty set is mathematically a subset of every policy, but it supplies no evidence of a compliant event.

\subsection{Report-bound protocol}
An administrator configures a task and its verifying key. The gateway snapshots the task configuration and rejects duplicated issuer keys or trust-domain labels. Labels enforce the administrator's enrollment decision; they do not prove independence of the underlying authorities. It issues a random 256-bit nonce with an expiry, using a trusted application clock. The vehicle and issuers derive
\begin{equation}
 c=\operatorname{OS2IP}\left(\operatorname{SHA256}(\operatorname{Encode}(v,T,\nu,e,\operatorname{SHA256}(R)))\right)\bmod q,
\end{equation}
\begin{sloppypar}
where $\operatorname{OS2IP}$ denotes octet-string-to-integer conversion, $T$ is the configured task, $R$ the report bytes, $\nu$ the nonce, $e$ the expiry, and $q$ the BN254 scalar-field modulus. The prototype serializes a fixed-order, map-free Go JSON structure containing version, task, challenge, and the lowercase hexadecimal SHA-256 report digest; issuer key byte arrays use Go's base64 JSON encoding. This deterministic prototype encoding is language-bound, so cross-language deployments should reproduce the exact bytes or replace it with a separately specified canonical encoding. SHA-256 reduction binds context; it is not encryption.
\end{sloppypar}

At submission the gateway checks the nonce and expiry, reconstructs the trusted public witness, verifies the proof, and accepts only the valid class. Successful verification atomically consumes the nonce; an eight-way replay test therefore admits one copy. Failed proofs leave the challenge reusable until expiry, enabling benign retries but also invalid-proof CPU exhaustion, so deployment requires rate limiting. Fresh challenges can still carry duplicate reports, and restart persistence or distributed atomicity is not implemented.

The result is a certificate whose meaning is inseparable from the authorized task and report. Reusing a geometrically valid witness under another report, threshold, roster, or challenge changes the public statement and therefore the proof obligation.

\subsection{Zero-knowledge proof construction}
The final step is to realize the same semantic relation inside a proof system. We implement two exact finite-domain constructions. Their cryptographic envelope is identical---the same signature checks, range constraints, context binding, and public decision code---but they establish threshold-supported reachability in different ways. This separation keeps the security statement fixed while allowing the geometric backend to match the operating regime.
\subsubsection{Lower-corner reachability}
For a query box $Q$, intersect each evidence box with $Q$. Let $A_a$ contain the lower endpoints of nonempty clipped boxes on axis $a$.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:corner}
If the maximum overlap depth in $Q$ is positive, some maximizer belongs to $A_1\times\cdots\times A_d$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a maximum-depth point $z$ and all boxes covering it. Their intersections with $Q$ have a common intersection. Its lower corner $v$ is formed by taking the greatest lower endpoint on each axis, hence $v_a\in A_a$. Every selected box contains $v$, so its depth is at least that of $z$ and therefore maximal.\end{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:corner} reduces exact reachability to finitely many lower-corner candidates. The circuit considers $n^d$ candidates, including duplicates, with coordinate $v_a=\max(\ell_{j_a a},Q^-_a)$. It tests query membership and coverage by every evidence box. Candidates associated with boxes not intersecting the query cannot cause false reachability because all membership comparisons are evaluated. Reachability is the OR of all candidate tests with depth at least $k$.

The integer complement $\Omega\setminus P$ is the union of at most $2d$ slabs. The low slab for axis $a$ ends at $p^-_a-1$ and the high slab begins at $p^+_a+1$. Other coordinates span the domain. Slabs can overlap. A slab beyond the domain boundary is inactive, with a safe selected endpoint before comparisons. The circuit computes $I$ over $P$ and $O$ over these slabs, then constrains the public code to
\begin{equation}
 \delta=(1-I)O+2IO+3(1-I)(1-O).
\end{equation}
All box coordinates are range-constrained, lower endpoints do not exceed upper endpoints, and boxes and policy lie within $\Omega$. Issuer signatures are checked inside the circuit. Pairwise public-key equality is forbidden.

\subsubsection{Exact backends and cost tradeoff}
For fixed bit width, the geometric circuit performs $O((2d+1)d n^{d+1})$ bounded comparisons and associated Boolean operations, besides signature checks and $O(n^2)$ key-distinctness tests. The cost is polynomial in $n$ for fixed $d$ and independent of the allowed omissions, but grows exponentially with dimension.

An alternative uses
\begin{equation}
 \F=\bigcup_{S\subseteq[n],\,|S|=k}\ \bigcap_{i\in S}U_i.
\end{equation}
Each intersection is itself a box, so nonemptiness, containment, and intersection with the policy can be checked directly. The native reference and a second ZK backend use $O\bigl(\binom nk kd\bigr)$ geometric operations for these tests. At $n=5,k=4$ and $d=3$, five subsets suffice, compared with $5^3$ lower-corner candidates per query. The subset circuit fixes $k$ at compilation and checks it against the public threshold. The depth circuit leaves that threshold public without fixing it at compilation. Both backends use the same signature verification, coordinate checks, and output semantics. Semantic evaluation uses the native subset implementation after equivalence testing; backend selection is explicit rather than automatically optimized.

Writing $b=n-k=f+r$ clarifies the parameter tradeoff: for fixed $b$, subset enumeration scales as $O(n^{b+1}d)$, while the depth construction scales as $O(n^{d+1})$ for fixed $d$. Thus a small fixed omission budget need not produce a crossover merely by increasing $n$; balanced $k\approx n/2$ instead makes the subset count exponential. As an explicit leading-order work proxy, define $W_D=(2d+1)dn^{d+1}$ and $W_S=\binom nk kd$. At $(n,d,k)=(20,3,19)$ these are 3,360,000 and 1,140; at $(20,3,10)$ they are 3,360,000 and 5,542,680. The depth construction has $n^d=8,000$ candidates per query at $n=20,d=3$, not $n^4$ candidates: each candidate still requires source-coverage tests. The proxies count unlike operations and omit implementation constants, so their ordering is not a measured constraint or runtime crossover. The current data justify subset enumeration for the tested regime only.

The lower-corner construction keeps the threshold public and scales polynomially in the number of sources for fixed dimension. Subset enumeration fixes the threshold at compilation but is considerably smaller when the omission budget $b=f+r$ is small. Rather than selecting one universally, the implementation exposes both backends and evaluates the regime in which each is practical.
\section{Security Analysis}\label{sec:security}
The preceding construction defines what the prover can certify; the security analysis determines what that certificate actually means. The key distinction is between guarantees enforced cryptographically and conclusions that still depend on the physical coverage of honest evidence. We make that boundary explicit before considering protocol attacks and privacy leakage.

\subsection{Threat model and security goals}
The vehicle and network are untrusted and may alter, replay, reorder, or substitute reports, proofs, decisions, and challenges. Byzantine issuers may choose arbitrary policy-aware boxes under their own keys. Honest issuers protect signing keys and bind evidence to the intended context, while the gateway's task configuration, verifying key, clock, and roster are trusted. The adversary is computationally bounded and is not assumed to break the underlying proof, signature, or hash primitives. Witness confidentiality is claimed against the verifier, not against evidence issuers or an administrator allowed unrestricted adaptive policy queries.


From this adversary model follow six properties that matter to the verifier: evidence authenticity, context integrity, conditional acceptance soundness, witness confidentiality, freshness, and fail-closed handling. Table~\ref{tab:security-properties} collects the assumptions and mechanisms behind each property so that the guarantee is not confused with broader deployment goals. In particular, the design does not claim source availability, physical calibration, anonymous networking, Sybil-resistant enrollment, unlinkability, or privacy against unrestricted adaptive policy queries; task policies are assumed to be administrator-authorized and challenge-bound.

These goals define the cryptographic boundary of the system. In particular, a proof can authenticate and hide evidence while still depending on the operational premise that enough honest boxes cover the true event.

\subsection{Correctness and soundness}
\begin{proposition}[Conditional evidence soundness]\label{prop:soundness}
If at most $f$ issuers are Byzantine and at most $r$ honest boxes miss $z^\star$, then $z^\star\in\F$. Consequently, a valid decision implies $z^\star\in P$, and an invalid decision implies $z^\star\notin P$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At least $n-f-r=k$ boxes contain the true state, so its coverage reaches the threshold in~\eqref{eq:feasible}. The two implications follow respectively from set containment and disjointness.\end{proof}
The proof uses the classical threshold-containment argument. The extra condition $n>2f+r$ guarantees at least one honest supporter for a threshold-supported point, but truth inclusion above does not require it.

For probabilistic calibration, let $M$ be the number of honest boxes missing the event and assume honest source $i$ has marginal failure probability at most $\alpha_i$ for the specified operating population. Without independence,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calibration}
 \Pr(M>r)\leq \min\left(1,\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal H}\alpha_i}{r+1}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed, $\mathbb{E}M\leq\sum_i\alpha_i$ and $M>r$ entails $M\geq r+1$. This loose bound does not supply missing calibration estimates. For adaptively chosen tasks, applicable conditional calibration or a suitable joint bound is required; a marginal guarantee from unrelated data is insufficient.

Under independent Bernoulli misses with known $\alpha_i$, a tighter Poisson-binomial tail is available, but shared positioning errors generally preclude using that bound. We therefore retain the dependence-agnostic guarantee in~\eqref{eq:calibration}.

Assuming the instantiated proof system's knowledge soundness, accepted proofs establish an assignment satisfying the signature and geometry relation. Signature unforgeability for authorized keys, collision resistance of the signed-box MiMC prehash and transcript-digest mapping, and correct context validation bind that assignment to authenticated evidence for the intended event. Proposition~\ref{prop:soundness} then connects that evidence to compliance on the stated physical-coverage event. A reduction would add cryptographic failure probabilities to the calibration-failure probability; this paper does not claim a mechanized end-to-end security proof.

Groth16 additionally requires a trustworthy circuit-specific setup: compromised trapdoor material can invalidate soundness. The benchmark setup is experimental only; deployment needs an appropriate ceremony or a proof system with a different setup model.

\begin{proposition}[Gateway-conditioned report acceptance]\label{prop:gateway}
Assume an authorized task/key configuration, correct gateway execution with a trustworthy clock, unforgeable issuer signatures, knowledge soundness of the proof system, and collision resistance of both the signed-box MiMC prehash and the instantiated transcript-digest mapping. If the gateway accepts a report and the physical coverage event in Proposition~\ref{prop:soundness} holds, the report's bound reference event satisfies the configured policy, except for cryptographic failure.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Verification uses public inputs reconstructed from the authorized task and the submitted report bytes. A sound accepted proof supplies correctly signed boxes for that context and the valid output. Replacing an honest issuer's signed box or its bound transcript while reusing a signature requires a prehash/transcript collision or a successful signature/proof forgery. The finite relation establishes a nonempty feasible set contained in the authorized policy. Proposition~\ref{prop:soundness} places the bound event in that set. The nonce store additionally prevents reuse of the same challenge after successful verification within that gateway instance.\end{proof}
The proposition assumes honest issuers associate their boxes with the same physical event and report context. It does not establish that the report content accurately describes the event. Its probabilistic physical failure term can be bounded by~\eqref{eq:calibration} only when the stated calibration assumptions are applicable.

To make the cryptographic boundary explicit, let $\mathsf{Acc}$ denote gateway acceptance, $\mathsf{Cov}$ the event that the Byzantine and honest-miscoverage budgets of Proposition~\ref{prop:soundness} hold, and $\mathsf{Comp}$ the event that the bound reference event lies in the configured policy. Let $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{snark}}$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sig}}$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{MiMC}}$, and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ctx}}$ upper-bound, for the concrete adversary resources under consideration, violation of Groth16 soundness/knowledge soundness, aggregate required-signature unforgeability, collision resistance of the MiMC signed-box prehash, and collision/binding failure of the deterministic $\operatorname{Encode}\rightarrow\operatorname{SHA256}\rightarrow\mathbb F_q$ context map. Let $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{state}}$ denote freshness-state failure.

\begin{proposition}[Composed conditional acceptance bound]\label{prop:composition}
Under the trusted task/roster/clock assumptions and the definitions above,
\begin{equation}
 \Pr[\mathsf{Acc}\wedge\mathsf{Cov}\wedge\neg\mathsf{Comp}]
 \leq
 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{snark}}+
 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{sig}}+
 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{MiMC}}+
 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ctx}}+
 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{state}}.
\end{equation}
In the modeled single-process gateway, atomic challenge consumption makes $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{state}}=0$ for same-instance replay; restart and multi-instance state are outside that model.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Assume none of the listed primitive or state failures occurs. Proof verification then implies a satisfying witness for the configured public statement; signature and context binding associate that witness with the authorized keys, report context, roster, threshold, challenge, and expiry. A valid classification establishes nonempty $\F\subseteq P$. On $\mathsf{Cov}$, Proposition~\ref{prop:soundness} gives $z^\star\in\F$, hence $z^\star\in P$, contradicting $\neg\mathsf{Comp}$. Therefore the bad event implies at least one listed failure, and the displayed inequality follows by a union bound.
\end{proof}

The implementation parameters are a 256-bit challenge nonce, SHA-256 for report/transcript hashing, Groth16 over BN254, and gnark's BN254 twisted-Edwards EdDSA with MiMC. We report these concrete choices rather than collapse heterogeneous assumptions into a single bit-security number; the implementation and library stack are not re-proved cryptographically in this work.

Taken together, Propositions~\ref{prop:soundness}--\ref{prop:composition} separate the guarantee into two layers. Cryptographic verification binds the proof to authenticated evidence and context; the source-coverage premise connects that certified evidence relation to the physical event. A failure of calibration therefore weakens the physical conclusion without turning a forged or substituted transcript into a valid proof.

\subsection{Resistance to protocol attacks}
Evidence tampering changes the signed-box message and therefore fails in-circuit signature verification. Report, task, roster, threshold, challenge, or expiry substitution changes the reconstructed context, so a proof created for one statement cannot be transplanted to another without breaking the assumed signature, hash, or proof primitives. Successful verification consumes the challenge atomically, which prevents same-challenge replay within one gateway instance; unknown or expired challenges fail before authorization. Missing roster evidence is treated as protocol abort rather than reinterpreted as an inconsistent-but-certified event.

Policy-aware collusion does not require a new geometric safety margin while the stated fault and coverage assumptions hold: regardless of how Byzantine boxes are chosen, $z^\star\in\F$ prevents an outside true event from yielding $\F\subseteq P$. The adversary can nevertheless reduce decisiveness or withhold signatures. Once the fault budget or coverage premise fails, the theorem no longer supplies a distribution-free correctness guarantee; any margin-based guarantee would require additional assumptions. The synthetic attacks already choose boxes using the true state and policy, rather than only adding random drift.

These mechanisms address integrity and replay, not availability. A Byzantine issuer can still withhold its signature, and a client can submit invalid proofs until a live challenge expires. Rate limiting, durable replay state, distributed atomicity, revocation, and partial-roster operation therefore remain system-layer concerns rather than properties derived from the circuit.

\subsection{Privacy and leakage}
Zero knowledge hides the private box coordinates and signatures from the verifier, but it does not hide every object associated with a report. In the implemented flow the gateway receives the report bytes $R$ in order to reconstruct the trusted public statement. The public context $c$ is a deterministic function of $\operatorname{SHA256}(R)$ and other task/challenge fields, so it is a binding value rather than an encryption mechanism or a hiding commitment. If $R$ is drawn from a low-entropy or enumerable space and the remaining context fields are known, a party that observes $c$ can test candidate reports offline by recomputing the map. We therefore make no report-content confidentiality claim against the gateway or against an observer who can enumerate candidate reports.

The public four-valued classification is itself an explicit leakage channel. A sequence of $q$ independently exposed four-valued answers has at most $4^q$ possible transcripts, i.e., at most $2q$ bits of output entropy; structured spatial queries can nevertheless use those bits efficiently to refine a location. This counting bound is not a privacy guarantee. The present design therefore assumes administrator-authorized, challenge-bound policies and does not claim privacy against unrestricted adaptive querying, anonymous-network protection, hidden roster membership, or unlinkable incentives. A deployment that needs those properties must add query authorization/rate or budget controls and a transport/identity layer outside the circuit.
\section{Experimental Evaluation}
The experiments follow the same distinction. We first ask whether the implementation enforces the intended statement under active protocol manipulation. We then study how the robust semantics behave as the coverage and Byzantine assumptions are satisfied or violated. Finally, we quantify the cost of proving the stronger authenticated-evidence relation and place that cost beside recent location-privacy systems without turning heterogeneous protocols into a speed ranking.

\subsection{Implementation and experimental setup}
The prototype is implemented in Go 1.25.6 with gnark 0.14.0~\cite{gnark}, BN254, Groth16~\cite{Groth2016}, and gnark's native twisted-Edwards EdDSA/MiMC primitives~\cite{MiMC2016}. The circuit selects \texttt{tedwards.BN254}; this construction is not Ed25519. Keys, signatures, setup, proving, and verification are all executed by the cryptographic implementation rather than emulated. BN254 is used to obtain reproducible prototype measurements; it is not presented as a post-quantum or long-term deployment choice.

The evaluation mirrors the two-layer argument of Section~\ref{sec:security}. Protocol tests attack the statement that the proof is supposed to bind; semantic experiments vary the quality of the evidence that makes that statement meaningful in the physical world. A third group of measurements then asks what it costs to prove the resulting relation. This organization keeps security failures, model failures, and performance costs analytically distinct rather than folding them into a single aggregate benchmark.

Proof benchmarks run on an eight-core Apple M1 Mac mini with 16~GB memory. Each circuit shape is set up once and then proved 30 times using a fixed signed witness. Reported proving times exclude compilation, evidence acquisition, networking, queueing, and energy. A deliberately weaker same-stack point-in-policy circuit provides a structural lower bound for generic hidden-point proving without issuer authentication or robust evidence semantics.

To characterize circuit growth independently of proving latency, we compile signed-box rank-1 constraint system (R1CS) instances for $n\in\{4,8,12,16\}$ and $d\in\{2,3\}$, using subset thresholds $k=n-1$, $n-2$, and $n/2$ together with one depth circuit per shape. The 32 seeded invocations use a 30~s timeout, a sampled 3~GiB resident-set-size (RSS) limit, and a 2500~MiB Go soft-memory target; resource-limited runs retain no estimated constraint counts.

The semantic study uses the domain $[0,63]^3$ and policy $[18,45]^3$ with five sources, Byzantine budget $f=1$, and baseline honest-miscoverage budget $r=0$. Twenty-seven conditions cross radii $\{2,4,6\}$, actual Byzantine counts $\{0,1,2\}$, and independent-calibrated, correlated-calibrated, or undercovered errors. Thirty seeds with 80 events each yield 64,800 base observations, 75\% of them near one policy face. Honest radii range from half the condition maximum to that maximum; Byzantine boxes are policy-aware rather than random. We compare the robust rule with a median point claim, majority-center voting, a fixed maximum-radius margin, and all-source intersection. Randomized equivalence checks independently verify the native subset classifier against depth-based and exhaustive formulations.

False acceptance is conditioned on outside events, false rejection on inside events, and decisive coverage counts valid or invalid outcomes. Each event also records $M$, the number of honest boxes missing the true state, which allows the theorem premise $M\le r$ to be tested directly. Re-evaluating the same 64,800 observations at $r=1$ and $r=2$ isolates the effect of the threshold $k=n-f-r$; across $r\in\{0,1,2\}$, this yields 194,400 matched evaluations without introducing new random samples.

Finally, the extracted UrbanNav-HK-Medium-Urban-1 reference sequence used in our replay contains 787 measured states spanning 786~s~\cite{UrbanNav}. After conversion to a local integer frame, a fixed percentile policy contains 169 states. Thirty replays add four simulated honest sources, one policy-aware Byzantine source, and bounded correlated error; a second mode multiplies the common bias by four. This experiment therefore tests decision behavior on a measured path, while keeping the evidence model explicitly simulated.

Together, the workloads let us ask whether protocol mutations fail closed, whether the semantics behave as predicted as their assumptions are satisfied or violated, and what cryptographic cost is paid for certifying that stronger relation.

\subsection{Discussion and limitations}

The evaluation supports the finite relation and its implementation, but it deliberately stops short of claiming a field-ready positioning system or an end-to-end crowdsensing platform. The implemented artifact is a certificate layer: a proof can certify how provisioned authenticated uncertainty boxes relate to a policy, but it cannot establish that those boxes are well calibrated, that nominal issuers are operationally independent, that participants were recruited or incentivized correctly, or that the report payload itself is true. The synthetic study controls source coverage by construction, while UrbanNav supplies a measured path with simulated evidence. Appendix~\ref{app:receiver} reinforces this distinction by showing that one retrospective calibration rule transfers poorly to a held-out segment. Real deployment therefore requires source- and regime-specific calibration together with governance for common-mode failures.

The fixed roster prevents selective evidence omission but allows one withholding issuer to abort. Distinct keys and trust-domain labels do not prove independent control. Durable replay state, distributed atomicity, revocation, anonymous networking, duplicate-report suppression, and rate limiting remain system-layer requirements.

Zero knowledge hides the signed-box witness from the verifier, not the report bytes received by the gateway, the public classification, roster, policy, threshold, or context; deterministic report binding also permits candidate testing when the report space is guessable. Repeated adaptive policies can leak additional location information. Performance measurements are workstation proof-kernel results, not OBU energy or end-to-end V2I latency. Groth16 also requires trusted setup. These limits bound deployment claims but do not change the finite relation proved by the circuit.

Accordingly, the zero-error calibrated simulations should be read as conditional consistency results, whereas Appendix~\ref{app:receiver} illustrates how the physical coverage premise can fail to transfer even within one recorded sequence.
\section{Conclusion}
This work reframes private spacetime verification around the evidence that supports a claim rather than around a single hidden coordinate. By retaining threshold-supported uncertainty and exposing valid, invalid, ambiguous, and inconsistent outcomes, the proposed relation avoids turning either convenient point selection or brittle all-source intersection into an authorization rule.

The resulting certificate layer is implemented with two Groth16 backends and a report-bound gateway, and is exercised against protocol attacks, calibrated and miscalibrated evidence, matched baselines, a pinned recent-work reproduction, and circuit-scaling workloads. The experiments support the finite evidence-certification relation and identify subset enumeration as the more practical backend in the tested small-omission regime. They also make the remaining boundary explicit: cryptography can bind and hide the signed-box witness, but physical multisource calibration, issuer independence, report-content truth, dynamic participation, and end-to-end crowdsensing availability must be established outside this layer.
