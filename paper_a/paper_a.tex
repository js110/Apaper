\begin{abstract}
Privacy-preserving location verification often proves a predicate over one hidden coordinate, even though the underlying event may be uncertain and some authenticated evidence sources may be malicious. We formulate robust spacetime compliance as a security relation over private signed uncertainty boxes. With $n$ enrolled issuers, Byzantine budget $f$, honest-miscoverage budget $r$, and threshold $k=n-f-r$, a report is authorized only when the nonempty set of states supported by at least $k$ boxes lies entirely inside the public policy. A four-valued decision separates valid and invalid outcomes from ambiguity and inconsistent evidence. We implement exact lower-corner and threshold-subset Groth16/BN254 circuits with in-circuit EdDSA/MiMC verification and bind proofs to the task, roster, report, challenge, threshold, and expiry. Gateway tests reject signed-evidence tampering, decision substitution, report/threshold substitution, expired or unknown challenges, and cross-challenge reuse; an eight-copy concurrent replay accepts exactly one copy. Across predefined calibrated synthetic conditions, robust certification provides 87.52\% mean decisive coverage versus 75.63\% for a fixed-margin rule. Subset circuits produce 164-byte proofs with 163--255~ms median proving time on an Apple M1 in the tested small-omission regime. The results validate the security relation and prototype, not field calibration: physical acceptance remains conditional on trusted enrollment and stated uncertainty-coverage assumptions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Privacy-preserving verification is often framed as proving that one hidden attribute satisfies a public predicate. For location-sensitive services, this can mean proving that a hidden coordinate lies in an authorized region without revealing the coordinate~\cite{ZKLP,VehiclePolicy}. The cryptographic statement can nevertheless be correct while the underlying physical claim is weakly supported: location and time estimates are uncertain, an authenticated source can be miscalibrated, and a compromised source can sign deliberately misleading evidence. The security question is therefore not only whether one hidden point satisfies a policy, but whether the available authenticated uncertainty evidence supports that policy robustly.

Vehicular crowdsensing provides a concrete instance of this problem. Mobile crowdsensing pools observations from participating devices~\cite{Suhag2023}, while vehicle-based systems use distributed sensing for transportation and environmental tasks~\cite{Zheng2025BARM}. A report may be eligible only when its reference event occurs in a specified region and time window, yet disclosing raw positioning evidence or a precise trajectory can expose sensitive mobility information. Near a policy boundary, selecting one convenient point from an uncertainty range can make an existential location proof succeed even when other plausible states violate the policy. Intersecting all source ranges is also unsafe under Byzantine evidence because a compromised source can exclude the true state while retaining a small compliant intersection.

We formulate the problem as a privacy-preserving security relation over authenticated uncertainty. Each enrolled issuer signs an axis-aligned integer box for the same report-bound reference event. With $n$ issuers, Byzantine budget $f$, honest-miscoverage budget $r$, and threshold $k=n-f-r$, the robust feasible set contains every state supported by at least $k$ signed boxes. A report is authorized only when this set is nonempty and entirely contained in the public policy. Separate reachability predicates inside and outside the policy distinguish valid, invalid, ambiguous, and inconsistent evidence instead of forcing every event into a binary decision.

The construction combines established fault-tolerant fusion ideas~\cite{Marzullo1990,BFTPoLoc,PG,CRSF} with zero-knowledge certification. The novelty is not a new SNARK primitive or a new sensor-fusion theorem. It is the security composition that turns robust uncertain evidence into a private, report-bound compliance certificate and makes the trust and calibration assumptions explicit.

The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
 \item We define robust spacetime-compliance semantics for signed uncertainty boxes under explicit Byzantine and honest-miscoverage budgets. The four-valued decision prevents vacuous acceptance and yields a conditional soundness statement that separates cryptographic validity from physical coverage assumptions.
 \item We give two exact finite-domain realizations of the relation: lower-corner maximum-depth reachability and threshold-subset enumeration. Both circuits verify issuer signatures in Groth16 and bind the private boxes to the public policy, roster, threshold, report context, challenge, and expiry.
 \item We implement a task-authorized gateway that reconstructs trusted public inputs, rejects roster/key inconsistencies, verifies the proof, and atomically consumes a challenge after successful verification. The resulting protocol addresses witness tampering, cross-task/report substitution, and same-challenge replay within the stated local model.
 \item We evaluate semantics and implementation separately. The study includes 64,800 synthetic observations, matched analyses across miscoverage budgets, a 787-state UrbanNav reference-trajectory replay with simulated evidence, 300 measured Groth16 proofs across two backends, and a resource-bounded compilation sweep. These experiments validate the implemented security relation and its cost; they are not presented as field calibration of real multisource vehicular positioning.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work and security positioning}
\subsection{Privacy-preserving authentication and vehicular security}
Anonymous participation need not rule out reliability checks. Cheng et al.'s AARE combines anonymous authentication with private reliability evaluation and reputation updates~\cite{Cheng2023}; Zheng et al.'s BARM adds blockchain-assisted identity management to anonymous authentication and reputation evaluation for sensing vehicles~\cite{Zheng2025BARM}. Zhou et al.'s PPVDA protects multidimensional sensing data using homomorphic message authentication codes and secret sharing, and verifies inner-product aggregation results~\cite{Zhou2024}. These mechanisms establish trust in participants or computations. They address a different question from whether a report's physical evidence supports its policy eligibility.

BCS-LPP, proposed by Shen et al., addresses worker location privacy and participation fairness~\cite{Shen2024}. It combines blockchain, Geohash encoding, order-preserving encryption, and deposits, with verification of submitted sensing locations. Sultana et al. associate suspicious identities through speed time series and roadside evidence fusion for cooperative Sybil detection~\cite{Sultana2024}. Our certificate takes an authorized roster and fault budget as inputs; it does not infer either from reputation or misbehavior detection. Given those inputs, it checks whether every threshold-supported event state satisfies the public policy.

Recent JISA work on vehicular security has emphasized private authentication and efficient verification. Imam and Li design lightweight anonymous certificateless aggregation for VANET authentication~\cite{Imam2025JISA}, while Wang et al. provide privacy-preserving certificateless aggregate signatures with invalid-signature detection~\cite{Wang2025JISA}. These systems protect identities and message authentication under vehicular threat models. Our target is orthogonal: after issuer identities and keys are provisioned, the verifier must decide whether private, uncertain physical evidence robustly supports a public spacetime policy. This distinction motivates treating authenticated evidence semantics, rather than authentication alone, as the certified object.

\subsection{Private location and zero-knowledge policy proofs}
Mdee et al. examine the effects of location-privacy mechanisms on vehicular applications~\cite{Mdee2022}. For electric-vehicle charging queries, Atmaca et al. use bounded location obfuscation and dummy queries to obtain approximate geo-indistinguishability~\cite{Atmaca2024}. Here the disclosed output is a policy decision rather than a perturbed coordinate. The privacy statement is therefore conditional on the public inputs and decision; it is not a differential-privacy guarantee.

Ghani et al. use physically unclonable functions, passwords, and randomized nonces for two-factor authentication in vehicular edge networks~\cite{Ghani2025}, while Zhang et al. address Sybil resistance through RSU-authorized short-term pseudonyms~\cite{Zhang2025Sybil}. Authenticating a participant still leaves the calibration of its evidence to be established.

Wang et al. bind polynomial commitments to identity in a blockchain and zero-knowledge design for anonymous vehicular authentication~\cite{Wang2025ZKAuth}. The proof concerns an authorized identity, not robust spatial support. Closer geometric formulations include ZKLP's accurate floating-point SNARKs for private geometric predicates~\cite{ZKLP} and vehicle subsidy and taxation protocols over signed trajectories~\cite{VehiclePolicy}. We use inclusive quantized boxes so that complement and threshold-coverage tests are exact under the stated coordinate convention.

\subsection{Robust evidence fusion and contribution boundary}
Marzullo's work establishes fault-tolerant fusion of uncertain continuous-valued sensors and supplies the threshold-fusion foundation used here~\cite{Marzullo1990}. BFT-PoLoc addresses the related problem of Byzantine-resistant geometric inference for Internet-delay location verification~\cite{BFTPoLoc}; it is not the source of our box-depth construction. Within our box model, subset enumeration and maximum-depth evaluation give equivalent reachability tests for the threshold-supported feasible set; the research focus is its certification against a public policy.

PG provides privacy-preserving Byzantine sensor fusion with guaranteed output delivery~\cite{PG}. The September 2026 CRSF preprint addresses collusion and manipulation of sensor participation through agreement and protected circuit-input release~\cite{CRSF}. Those distributed liveness guarantees are absent from our fixed-roster prototype. Its output is instead a portable proof of a public policy classification, with evidence boxes and signatures kept private. We compare these interfaces, not performance: neither PG nor CRSF was reimplemented under a matched workload.

\section{Evidence and adversary model}
The event reference state is $z^\star=(x,y,t)$, or more generally a point in a finite domain
\begin{equation}
 \Omega=\prod_{a=1}^{d}[L_a,H_a]\cap\mathbb{Z}^d.
\end{equation}
A policy is an integer box $P=(\prod_a[p^-_a,p^+_a])\cap\mathbb{Z}^d\subseteq\Omega$. Each issuer $i$ signs an evidence box $U_i=(\prod_a[\ell_{ia},u_{ia}])\cap\mathbb{Z}^d\subseteq\Omega$ associated with a common public context $c$. All endpoints are integers, and subsequent box operations use this inclusive integer convention. Coordinates use a public reference frame, epoch, units, and quantization rule. In the prototype, coordinate bounds are unsigned 16-bit integers. Exactness in this paper refers to this finite model; a deployment must conservatively handle physical values falling within a boundary cell.

An issuer signature authenticates the submitted bounds. It does not prove their physical accuracy. We assume at most $f$ Byzantine issuers and at most $r$ additional honest boxes that miss $z^\star$. Honest errors may be dependent. Distinct public keys are necessary but do not establish distinct trust domains: the deployment must reject multiple registered keys controlled by the same counted authority. Sybil-resistant vehicular authentication likewise requires controls on pseudonym authorization~\cite{Zhang2025Sybil}; in our model, the counted unit is an enrolled authority, not an arbitrary key. Several estimates derived from one GNSS receiver cannot be counted as independent authorities simply because they have different field names.

The vehicle may submit false witnesses, replay signatures, modify evidence, or withhold reports. An edge verifier checks proofs and public task inputs. Every roster slot must be present for the relation to hold. A missing signature causes a protocol abort; it is not the same as a proved evidence-failure decision over a complete set of signed boxes. The prototype supplies no availability guarantee against withholding.

\paragraph{Trust boundary and adversary capabilities}
The reporting vehicle and the communication path are untrusted: an adversary may alter, replay, reorder, or substitute submitted reports, proofs, decisions, and challenges. Up to $f$ enrolled issuers may be Byzantine and may choose policy-aware boxes and signatures arbitrarily under their own keys. Honest issuers are assumed to protect their signing keys and to bind their boxes to the intended event/context, but up to $r$ honest boxes may miss the true event. The gateway's task configuration, verifying key, application clock, and administrator-defined roster are trusted in the present model. The adversary is computationally bounded and is not assumed to break the underlying signature, hash, or proof-system security. Confidentiality is claimed against the proof verifier for private witness fields; it is not claimed against the issuers that created those fields or against an administrator permitted to issue unrestricted adaptive policies.

The public statement contains $c$, $\Omega$, $P$, $k$, a decision code, and the ordered issuer keys. Issuers sign
\begin{equation}
 m_i=\operatorname{MiMC}(c,i,\ell_{i1},u_{i1},\ldots,\ell_{id},u_{id}).
\end{equation}
The signed context binds the protocol version, task and circuit identifiers, domain, policy, threshold, and roster. It also includes the coordinate convention, calibration profile, report digest, challenge nonce, and expiry. The gateway reconstructs these inputs from its configured task rather than accepting a vehicle-selected public witness. This gateway is a local integration harness with an in-memory challenge store, without distributed enrollment, durable freshness storage, or a networked task registry. The microbenchmark measures only the proof kernel.

\paragraph{Physical evidence interface}
A deployable issuer would need to identify the same event, transform its observation into the configured frame and epoch, conservatively enclose spatial and timing uncertainty, attach a validated calibration profile, and sign the resulting box and context. Candidate integration designs include an operator-controlled radio-positioning service, a roadside perception operator, and an independently administered ranging anchor. These are proposed interfaces, not sources integrated or validated in this study. Radio links do not by themselves provide calibrated ranging, and roadside object association, non-line-of-sight bias, synchronization, and frame registration must be included in the uncertainty model. A vehicle-controlled sensor stack cannot be treated as several honest authorities against its owner merely by assigning different keys; its compromise must be covered by the fault budget. A shared operator or calibration failure can compromise several nominal sources simultaneously.

\paragraph{Withholding and partial participation}
One withholding roster member can abort the current protocol. This is a liveness limitation, not a failure of the conditional acceptance theorem. A possible extension would authorize a registry of $N$ issuers, bind an active set $S$ of size $m$ to the task context, prove membership and distinctness, and use $k_S=m-f_S-r_S\geq1$. Merely accepting any $m\geq k$ signatures while retaining the old threshold does not ensure that $k$ honest covering boxes remain. For an adaptively selected set, the coverage and fault assumptions must hold for that selection, not just for an unrelated population. Prover-controlled omissions can also change a decision, so selection rules and availability reporting require explicit governance. With a Merkle registry, a padded implementation would add membership-path hashing proportional to $m\log N$, active-slot constraints and distinctness checks, besides the geometry and signatures. Actual constraint counts and liveness depend on a new implementation and delivery model; neither is evaluated here. The fixed-roster prototype therefore targets provisioned, delay-tolerant report checks rather than opportunistic safety-critical V2X control.

\paragraph{Conservative physical boundaries}
For a physical grid cell $C(z)$, evidence indices should include every cell intersecting the physical uncertainty region, while acceptance should use only cells wholly contained in the physical policy. Then an accepted nonempty feasible set cannot accept a boundary-straddling cell as wholly compliant. This can increase abstention or conservative rejection. Conversely, a physical invalidity claim requires disjointness from every cell intersecting the policy; disjointness from an inward-rounded policy alone is insufficient. Preserving all four physical meanings may therefore require separate inner/outer policy approximations or a boundary-ambiguity rule. The implemented single-policy integer relation does not supply this continuous-to-discrete adapter; values beyond the configured range must be rejected rather than silently clamped.

\subsection{Report binding and challenge lifecycle}
An administrator configures a task and its verifying key. The gateway snapshots the task configuration and rejects duplicated issuer keys or trust-domain labels. Labels enforce the administrator's enrollment decision; they do not prove independence of the underlying authorities. It issues a random 256-bit nonce with an expiry, using a trusted application clock. The vehicle and issuers derive
\begin{equation}
 c=\operatorname{OS2IP}\left(\operatorname{SHA256}(\operatorname{Encode}(v,T,\nu,e,\operatorname{SHA256}(R)))\right)\bmod q,
\end{equation}
\begin{sloppypar}
where $T$ is the complete configured task, $R$ is the report byte string, $\nu$ is the nonce, $e$ is its expiry, and $q$ is the BN254 scalar-field modulus. The prototype serializes a Go struct with fields \texttt{Version}, \texttt{Task}, \texttt{Challenge}, and \texttt{ReportSHA256} in declaration order using \texttt{encoding/json}; the version literal is \texttt{PCVCS-PaperA-\allowbreak context-v1}. The task struct contains the task/circuit identifiers, coordinate convention, calibration profile, domain and policy bounds, bit width, threshold, and ordered roster. A generated challenge contains a 32-byte random nonce encoded as 64 hexadecimal characters and an integer expiry; the report field is the lowercase hexadecimal SHA-256 digest of the exact report bytes. Go JSON encodes issuer public-key byte arrays as base64 strings. This executable encoding is deterministic for the current map-free structs, but it is language-bound: a cross-language implementation must reproduce the same bytes or replace it with a separately specified canonical encoding. Every issuer signs its box against the resulting $c$. SHA-256 reduction is domain binding, not encryption; report-digest dictionary attacks remain possible for low-entropy reports.
\end{sloppypar}

At submission the gateway looks up the nonce, checks expiry, reconstructs the context and public witness from the trusted configuration and received report, and verifies the proof. Only a valid decision authorizes report acceptance. Every successfully verified decision consumes its nonce. Verification and nonce consumption are serialized under a mutex, so racing copies cannot both succeed within one gateway process. Failed proofs leave the nonce available until expiry. This implementation does not prevent a vehicle from requesting another challenge for the same report, and therefore supplies challenge replay protection rather than identity-based duplicate-report suppression. Restart persistence, distributed atomicity and challenge-rate limiting remain deployment tasks.

\subsection{Security objectives and non-goals}
The protocol is evaluated against five explicit security objectives. \emph{Evidence authenticity and context integrity} require that an accepted witness contain boxes signed by the configured issuer keys and bound to the same task, report, challenge, expiry, coordinate convention, policy, threshold, and roster. \emph{Conditional acceptance soundness} requires that a valid proof authorize a report only when the certified robust relation is satisfied; connecting that relation to the physical event additionally requires the source-coverage assumption formalized in Proposition~\ref{prop:soundness}. \emph{Witness confidentiality} requires the verifier to learn no box coordinates or signatures beyond what follows from the public statement and decision, relying on the zero-knowledge property of the instantiated proof system. \emph{Freshness} requires successful verification to consume the issued challenge so that the same challenge cannot be replayed within the gateway instance. \emph{Fail-closed evidence handling} requires missing signatures, malformed public inputs, wrong decisions, expired challenges, and inconsistent rosters to abort rather than be reinterpreted as compliant evidence.

These objectives do not provide source availability, physical calibration, anonymous networking, Sybil-resistant real-world authority enrollment, or unlinkability across accepted reports. They also do not authorize a verifier to issue arbitrarily many adaptive geofences against the same event. The intended deployment model therefore restricts verification to administrator-authorized task policies already bound into the challenge context. Rate limiting, audit logging, and policy-granularity controls would be required if task creation itself were exposed to an adversarial verifier; those controls are outside the present prototype.
\section{Robust compliance semantics}
For $1\leq k=n-f-r\leq n$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:feasible}
 \F=\{z\in\Omega:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ind[z\in U_i]\geq k\}.
\end{equation}
Let $I=\ind[\F\cap P\neq\varnothing]$ and $O=\ind[\F\cap(\Omega\setminus P)\neq\varnothing]$. These two bits give all four outcomes in Table~\ref{tab:decisions}. The inconsistent outcome is called evidence failure in the implementation and result tables. Distinguishing it avoids vacuous acceptance: the empty set is mathematically a subset of every policy, but it supplies no evidence of a compliant event.

In the constructed example of Figure~\ref{fig:counterexample}, $\Omega=[-4,8]\cap\mathbb Z$ and both honest intervals contain $z^\star=-1$. The Byzantine interval excludes it. The all-source intersection $[2,4]\cap\mathbb Z$ lies inside the policy even though it excludes the true state, and a conveniently chosen point also passes. Threshold coverage retains $\F=[-2,4]\cap\mathbb Z$, giving an ambiguous decision. The example applies the existing fusion rationale; it supplies neither a new theorem nor an experimental success rate.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional evidence soundness]\label{prop:soundness}
If at most $f$ issuers are Byzantine and at most $r$ honest boxes miss $z^\star$, then $z^\star\in\F$. Consequently, a valid decision implies $z^\star\in P$, and an invalid decision implies $z^\star\notin P$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At least $n-f-r=k$ boxes contain the true state, so its coverage reaches the threshold in~\eqref{eq:feasible}. The two implications follow respectively from set containment and disjointness.\end{proof}
The proof uses the classical threshold-containment argument. The extra condition $n>2f+r$ guarantees at least one honest supporter for a threshold-supported point, but truth inclusion above does not require it.

For probabilistic calibration, let $M$ be the number of honest boxes missing the event and assume honest source $i$ has marginal failure probability at most $\alpha_i$ for the specified operating population. Without independence,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calibration}
 \Pr(M>r)\leq \min\left(1,\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal H}\alpha_i}{r+1}\right).
\end{equation}
Indeed, $\mathbb{E}M\leq\sum_i\alpha_i$ and $M>r$ entails $M\geq r+1$. This loose bound does not supply missing calibration estimates. For adaptively chosen tasks, applicable conditional calibration or a suitable joint bound is required; a marginal guarantee from unrelated data is insufficient.

If honest misses were independently Bernoulli with known probabilities $\alpha_i$, the tighter reference probability would be the upper tail of the Poisson-binomial distribution, obtained from coefficients of $\prod_i[(1-\alpha_i)+\alpha_i z]$. For five hypothetical honest sources with $\alpha_i=0.05$, the independence-based tails for $r=0,1,2$ are 0.226219, 0.0225925, and 0.00115813, versus the dependence-agnostic bounds 0.25, 0.125, and 0.0833333. Thus the gap is modest at $r=0$ but large for positive budgets. These are analytical illustrations, not fitted failure probabilities; shared positioning errors do not justify substituting an independence-based bound for the dependence-agnostic guarantee.

\section{Finite proof construction}
\subsection{Lower-corner reachability}
For a query box $Q$, intersect each evidence box with $Q$. Let $A_a$ contain the lower endpoints of nonempty clipped boxes on axis $a$.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:corner}
If the maximum overlap depth in $Q$ is positive, some maximizer belongs to $A_1\times\cdots\times A_d$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a maximum-depth point $z$ and all boxes covering it. Their intersections with $Q$ have a common intersection. Its lower corner $v$ is formed by taking the greatest lower endpoint on each axis, hence $v_a\in A_a$. Every selected box contains $v$, so its depth is at least that of $z$ and therefore maximal.\end{proof}
The circuit considers $n^d$ candidates, including duplicates, with coordinate $v_a=\max(\ell_{j_a a},Q^-_a)$. It tests query membership and coverage by every evidence box. Candidates associated with boxes not intersecting the query cannot cause false reachability because all membership comparisons are evaluated. Reachability is the OR of all candidate tests with depth at least $k$.

The integer complement $\Omega\setminus P$ is the union of at most $2d$ slabs. The low slab for axis $a$ ends at $p^-_a-1$ and the high slab begins at $p^+_a+1$. Other coordinates span the domain. Slabs can overlap. A slab beyond the domain boundary is inactive, with a safe selected endpoint before comparisons. The circuit computes $I$ over $P$ and $O$ over these slabs, then constrains the public code to
\begin{equation}
 \delta=(1-I)O+2IO+3(1-I)(1-O).
\end{equation}
All box coordinates are range-constrained, lower endpoints do not exceed upper endpoints, and boxes and policy lie within $\Omega$. Issuer signatures are checked inside the circuit. Pairwise public-key equality is forbidden.

\subsection{Cost and competing exact algorithms}
For fixed bit width, the geometric circuit performs $O((2d+1)d n^{d+1})$ bounded comparisons and associated Boolean operations, besides signature checks and $O(n^2)$ key-distinctness tests. The cost is polynomial in $n$ for fixed $d$ and independent of the allowed omissions, but grows exponentially with dimension.

An alternative uses
\begin{equation}
 \F=\bigcup_{S\subseteq[n],\,|S|=k}\ \bigcap_{i\in S}U_i.
\end{equation}
Each intersection is itself a box, so nonemptiness, containment, and intersection with the policy can be checked directly. The native reference and a second ZK backend use $O\bigl(\binom nk kd\bigr)$ geometric operations for these tests. At $n=5,k=4$, five subsets suffice, compared with $5^3$ lower-corner candidates per query. The subset circuit fixes $k$ at compilation and checks it against the public threshold. The depth circuit leaves that threshold public without fixing it at compilation. Both backends use the same signature verification, coordinate checks, and output semantics. Semantic evaluation uses the native subset implementation after equivalence testing; backend selection is explicit rather than automatically optimized.

Writing $b=n-k=f+r$ clarifies the parameter tradeoff: for fixed $b$, subset enumeration scales as $O(n^{b+1}d)$, while the depth construction scales as $O(n^{d+1})$ for fixed $d$. Thus a small fixed omission budget need not produce a crossover merely by increasing $n$; balanced $k\approx n/2$ instead makes the subset count exponential. As an explicit leading-order work proxy, define $W_D=(2d+1)dn^{d+1}$ and $W_S=\binom nk kd$. At $(n,d,k)=(20,3,19)$ these are 3,360,000 and 1,140; at $(20,3,10)$ they are 3,360,000 and 5,542,680. The depth construction has $n^d=8,000$ candidates per query at $n=20,d=3$, not $n^4$ candidates: each candidate still requires source-coverage tests. The proxies count unlike operations and omit implementation constants, so their ordering is not a measured constraint or runtime crossover. The current data justify subset enumeration for the tested regime only.

\subsection{Security and leakage boundary}
Assuming the instantiated proof system's knowledge soundness, accepted proofs establish an assignment satisfying the signature and geometry relation. Signature unforgeability for authorized keys, collision resistance of the signed-box MiMC prehash and transcript-digest mapping, and correct context validation bind that assignment to authenticated evidence for the intended event. Proposition~\ref{prop:soundness} then connects that evidence to compliance on the stated physical-coverage event. A reduction would add cryptographic failure probabilities to the calibration-failure probability; this paper does not claim a mechanized end-to-end security proof.

The zero-knowledge property hides box coordinates and signatures from the verifier, conditional on the public statement. The roster, domain, policy, threshold, context, and classification are public. The prover sees the boxes it receives, and issuing authorities retain their observations. Repeated linkable classifications can reveal additional location information. In particular, a verifier allowed to adaptively vary fine-grained policies could use the disclosed classifications as an oracle that narrows the event location. The prototype therefore treats the administrator-authorized task policy as part of the trust boundary and does not provide privacy against unrestricted adaptive policy queries, anonymous networking, private roster membership, or unlinkable rewards. Its privacy claim is limited to the proof witness under the configured public statement.

Policy-aware collusion does not require a new geometric safety margin while the stated fault and coverage assumptions hold: regardless of how Byzantine boxes are chosen, $z^\star\in\F$ prevents an outside true event from yielding $\F\subseteq P$. The adversary can nevertheless reduce decisiveness or withhold signatures. Once the budget or coverage event fails, no fixed policy margin restores a distribution-free guarantee. The synthetic attacks already choose boxes using the true state and policy, rather than only adding random drift.

\paragraph{Reward deduplication is a separate relation}
Anonymous membership protocols such as Semaphore provide scoped double-signaling prevention~\cite{SemaphoreDocs}. A potential reward extension would prove ownership of an enrolled secret $s$ and compute a domain-separated nullifier $\eta=\operatorname{PRF}_s(\text{task},\text{epoch})$ in the same report-bound proof. Its scope must not include a fresh challenge, which would permit a new nullifier on every retry. The reward service must atomically enforce uniqueness and persist that state. This prevents repeated rewards per enrolled credential and scope, not Sybil enrollment: freely chosen pseudonym keys defeat it. Unique-credential issuance, renewal, revocation, permitted reporting frequency, and the resulting within-scope linkability are additional assumptions. A prover-selected grid identifier is likewise unsafe unless policy-bound and proven. No nullifier or incentive mechanism is implemented or benchmarked here.

\begin{proposition}[Gateway-conditioned report acceptance]
Assume an authorized task/key configuration, correct gateway execution with a trustworthy clock, unforgeable issuer signatures, knowledge soundness of the proof system, and collision resistance of both the signed-box MiMC prehash and the instantiated transcript-digest mapping. If the gateway accepts a report and the physical coverage event in Proposition~\ref{prop:soundness} holds, the report's bound reference event satisfies the configured policy, except for cryptographic failure.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Verification uses public inputs reconstructed from the authorized task and the submitted report bytes. A sound accepted proof supplies correctly signed boxes for that context and the valid output. Replacing an honest issuer's signed box or its bound transcript while reusing a signature requires a prehash/transcript collision or a successful signature/proof forgery. The finite relation establishes a nonempty feasible set contained in the authorized policy. Proposition~\ref{prop:soundness} places the bound event in that set. The nonce store additionally prevents reuse of the same challenge after successful verification within that gateway instance.\end{proof}
The proposition assumes honest issuers associate their boxes with the same physical event and report context. It does not establish that the report content accurately describes the event. Its probabilistic physical failure term can be bounded by~\eqref{eq:calibration} only when the stated calibration assumptions are applicable.

\section{Implementation and experimental design}
The ZK prototype uses Go 1.25.6, gnark 0.14.0~\cite{gnark}, BN254, Groth16~\cite{Groth2016}, and gnark's twisted-Edwards EdDSA with MiMC~\cite{MiMC2016}. This EdDSA instantiation differs from Ed25519. Keys and signatures use real cryptographic randomness, and the benchmark runs compilation, setup, witness construction, proving, and verification without placeholder cryptography.

The evaluation is organized around four questions: \textbf{RQ1}, does the implemented proof/gateway reject statement tampering and replay; \textbf{RQ2}, how much decisive coverage is retained by robust semantics relative to point and margin baselines when the stated assumptions hold or fail; \textbf{RQ3}, how does the honest-miscoverage budget change the availability--error tradeoff; and \textbf{RQ4}, what proof, verification, and circuit-growth costs arise for the two exact backends?

\paragraph{AI-assisted coding disclosure}
OpenAI Codex was used as a coding assistant for portions of analysis/plotting code and repository-level checks. Reported measurements, simulation outputs, and plotted values were produced from the executable code and saved experimental records; generative AI was not used to fabricate or alter experimental result values. The resulting code paths were exercised by the reproducibility tests and artifact audits reported in this study.

Backend tests verify four decision fixtures and reject wrong decisions, modified signed bounds, and duplicated public keys. Integration tests additionally sign the full field context and pass real proofs through the gateway. Genuine proofs for all four decision classes verify and consume their challenges, but only the valid class authorizes a report. All twelve unequal proof/code pairings are rejected, as are report replacement, expiry, unknown nonces, cross-challenge reuse, and threshold substitution. A missing proof leaves the challenge unconsumed. In an eight-way concurrent replay, exactly one copy is accepted; duplicate trust-domain registration is also rejected. These are regression tests, not exhaustive cryptographic validation.

Measurements use a Mac mini with an eight-core Apple M1, 16~GB memory, and macOS 26.6.2. For each circuit shape, one randomized setup is followed by 30 sequential proofs of a fixed signed witness. Setup is timed once and excluded from online times. The quantiles describe repetitions within that process, with p99 especially imprecise for 30 measurements. Proof-generation latency excludes compilation, witness construction, signature collection, network transfer, and queueing. OBU energy and end-to-end V2I latency were not measured.

An end-to-end submission would have
\begin{equation}
 T_{\mathrm{total}}=T_{\mathrm{challenge}}+T_{\mathrm{evidence}}+T_{\mathrm{witness}}+T_{\mathrm{prove}}+T_{\mathrm{upload}}+T_{\mathrm{verify}}+T_{\mathrm{queue/store}}.
\end{equation}
The benchmark times local witness construction and the proof kernel. The other terms---challenge exchange, physical evidence collection, wireless transport, and service queue/storage---remain unmeasured. Parallel issuer requests would still wait for the slowest required response; a withholding roster member causes an abort at timeout. Similarly, the 164-byte proof and 556--748-byte public witness account only for the cryptographic components of a wire packet. M1 workstation timings are not extrapolated to an OBU and establish neither embedded-device feasibility nor a real-time deadline.

Experimental figures are generated reproducibly from recorded results using Python and Matplotlib. OpenAI Codex (OpenAI; model version not exposed in the retained workflow record) assisted preparation of the analysis and plotting code. The plots are data-derived renderings, not image-model outputs; source-data tables, rendering scripts, and artifact hashes accompany the draft.

\subsection{Resource-bounded circuit compilation}
The compilation sweep uses 4, 8, 12, and 16 issuers in two and three dimensions, with 16-bit coordinates. It compiles the complete signed-box R1CS. Subset thresholds cover $k=n-1$, $n-2$, and $n/2$; coincident settings at $n=4$ are run once. The depth circuit retains its public threshold and is compiled once per shape. Two $(n,d)=(5,2)$ controls reproduce prior counts, giving 32 separate process invocations in a seeded randomized order (seed 20260915). One compilation is performed per configuration: constraint counts are structural measurements, while compile times are descriptive single runs. Each child process has a 30~s timeout, a sampled RSS termination threshold of 3~GiB at 50~ms polling intervals, and a Go soft memory target of 2500~MiB. Setup and proof generation are excluded. Failed runs retain their logs and resource status without estimated constraint counts. The sweep uses the same workstation and gnark version as the proof benchmark.

\subsection{Synthetic semantic experiment}
The domain is $[0,63]^3$ and the policy is $[18,45]^3$. We use five sources, $f=1$, and $r=0$. Twenty-seven conditions cross three maximum radii (2, 4, 6), three actual Byzantine counts (0, 1, 2), and three error models. Each condition contains 30 seeds with 80 events per seed, for 64,800 event-condition observations. A seed is the experimental unit. Seventy-five percent of event generation chooses one axis near a policy face; the other events are uniform. This face-biased construction can include points far from the actual policy boundary on other axes.

Honest box radii vary between half the maximum radius and that maximum, rounded to integers. Independent calibrated errors lie within the declared radius. Correlated calibrated errors share a bounded direction and have bounded jitter. A misspecified model increases the common error scale beyond the declared radius. Byzantine boxes equal the policy for an outside event, or select the nearest outside slab for an inside event. These attacks are deliberately informed by truth and policy; their distribution is not an estimate of real adversarial traffic.

Baselines are median-center point classification, majority voting on center compliance, a fixed maximum-radius box around the median center, and intersection of all sources. The robust native subset classifier provides the proposed semantic output. We also compare subset and depth outputs in 1,200 randomized tests across one, two, and three dimensions, and compare maximum depth with exhaustive grid enumeration in a further 1,200 tests. The baselines isolate semantic choices; they are not replicas of PG, CRSF, or BFT-PoLoc.

False acceptance and false rejection use different denominators: outside events for acceptance, inside events for invalid classification. Decisive coverage is the fraction of all events classified valid or invalid; ambiguity and evidence failure are reported separately. Confidence intervals are normal approximations over seed-level rates, describing simulation variability rather than a population guarantee of zero error. Raw outcome counts and class denominators are retained for each seed. When a denominator is zero, that conditional rate is undefined and omitted from its seed average, with the number of defined seeds recorded. The seed still contributes to other metrics.

\subsection{Miscoverage-budget ablation}
We repeat the 27-condition synthetic experiment with $r=1$ and $r=2$, preserving the random seeds and event-generation logic. Together with $r=0$, these are 194,400 event-condition evaluations, but only 64,800 distinct randomized base observations. The paired runs isolate the classification effect of changing $k$ from four to three to two. They use the native exact classifier; the cryptographic table remains a $k=n-1$ benchmark. These runs do not fit or estimate empirical calibration parameters.

\subsection{Trajectory replay}
We downloaded the UrbanNav-HK-Medium-Urban-1 reference file from the official dataset links~\cite{UrbanNav}. The file supplies 787 timestamped states over 786~s. Latitude and longitude are converted from degrees/minutes/seconds to a local equirectangular frame using an Earth radius of 6,371,000~m; horizontal coordinates and elapsed seconds are rounded to integers with positive offsets. Policy bounds are the per-axis 20th and 80th percentiles, fixed before evidence replay; 169 reference states satisfy this policy.

For each of 30 evidence realizations on this single trajectory, four honest sources have simulated spatial radii of 8--20~m and temporal radii of 1--3~s. Shared errors follow a bounded autoregressive process with coefficient 0.9 and source-specific bounded jitter. One Byzantine source targets the opposite policy outcome. For inside states, this replay attack uses the nearest lower policy face. A second mode multiplies the common bias by four to test misspecification. These are simulated evidence processes on a measured reference path, not measured GNSS residuals or independent real authorities. Replication estimates uncertainty conditional on the one path and policy, and supports no between-route generalization.

\section{Limitations and deployment implications}
The security claim is deliberately narrower than a claim of field-ready vehicular positioning. The proof certifies the relation between authenticated uncertainty boxes and a public policy; it does not establish that the boxes are physically calibrated, that nominally distinct issuers are independent real-world trust domains, or that the report payload itself is truthful. These are input-trust and deployment assumptions rather than hidden properties of the ZK circuit.

The strongest unresolved external-validity issue is uncertainty calibration. The synthetic experiments control whether honest boxes cover the reference event, and the UrbanNav experiment replays simulated evidence over a measured trajectory. Appendix~\ref{app:receiver} intentionally shows that one retrospective calibration prescription transfers poorly even within a single static receiver sequence. The manuscript therefore does not infer real multisource coverage rates from these experiments. A field deployment would need held-out calibration for each evidence source and operating regime, plus governance for common-mode failures and shared operators.

Full-roster signatures prevent a prover from silently dropping inconvenient evidence, but they also allow one withholding issuer to block completion. Distinct keys and configured trust-domain labels enforce the administrator's roster decision without proving physical independence or preventing Sybil enrollment outside that administrative process. Durable replay state, distributed atomicity, source availability, revocation, anonymous networking, and reward deduplication remain separate system problems.

Zero knowledge protects the private witness, not every inference from the public output. The classification, roster, policy, threshold, and context are public. Repeated or adaptively chosen policies can leak additional location information; the present model therefore assumes administrator-authorized task policies and does not claim privacy against an unrestricted policy-query oracle.

The performance study measures real proof generation and verification but not an automotive deployment. It uses one Apple M1 workstation, small issuer rosters, and local challenge handling. Network transfer, evidence acquisition, queueing, embedded-device energy, and end-to-end V2I latency remain unmeasured. Groth16 also requires a trusted-setup lifecycle for each circuit shape. The compilation sweep identifies resource boundaries but does not convert censored runs into performance estimates.

These limitations do not change the finite security relation proved by the circuit. They delimit what that relation says about a physical deployment: cryptographic acceptance is meaningful only when enrollment, event binding, and the stated source-coverage assumptions are applicable.

\section{Conclusion}
This work addresses a security gap between proving a hidden point claim and certifying what uncertain, potentially Byzantine evidence actually supports. The proposed relation authorizes a report only when every threshold-supported state lies inside the task policy, while separately representing invalid, ambiguous, and inconsistent evidence.

Two Groth16 implementations evaluate the same finite semantics while keeping signed evidence boxes private. The report-bound gateway links the proof to an authorized task, report, challenge, expiry, threshold, and roster, and the prototype demonstrates tamper rejection and same-challenge replay protection within its local execution model. In the measured small-omission regime, subset enumeration is substantially cheaper than the lower-corner depth construction, while the semantic experiments show how uncertainty tolerance trades decisiveness against robustness when assumptions are stressed.

The contribution is therefore a privacy-preserving certification mechanism with explicit trust boundaries, not a claim that real-world positioning uncertainty has already been solved. The next empirical step is independent multisource calibration; the present result supplies the security relation, implementation, and reproducible cost evidence that such a deployment can build upon.

\section{Retrospective single-receiver calibration diagnostic}\label{app:receiver}
We analyze reference positions and precomputed RTKLIB outputs from GraphGNSSLib's TST2 directory~\cite{GraphGNSSData}, a static sequence separate from the moving UrbanNav replay. The analysis records a pinned revision and source hashes. Its constant reference position has 157 epochs, GPS week 2108, seconds 270149--270305; all exactly match solution epochs. The remaining 829 solution epochs are excluded without reference extrapolation.

The header specifies RTKLIB 2.4.2 and kinematic, combined processing. Horizontal residuals use WGS84 Earth-centered coordinates projected east--north at the reference, without fitted alignment, bias correction, quality filtering, or interpolation. Vertical residuals are excluded because reference height and antenna conventions are unverified. This analyzes published outputs against the repository reference; it neither reproduces the estimator nor independently surveys positioning errors.

The first 78 matched epochs form a calibration segment, followed by ten omitted epochs and 69 evaluation epochs. Before computing residual summaries, we recorded this exploratory recipe: let $s_j=\max(|e_j|,|n_j|)$ and choose the $\lceil(78+1)0.95\rceil=76$th ordered calibration score as a fixed square half-width. The resulting radius is 0.863506~m. We also evaluate rectangles with east/north half-widths of three times the corresponding standard deviations reported in the solution file. This multiplier is a diagnostic choice, not an asserted simultaneous coverage level.

The fixed square contains 97.44\% of calibration references but only 2.90\% of evaluation references (Table~\ref{tab:receiver}). Median horizontal residual increases from 0.869 to 1.038~m; the largest evaluation residual is 1.522~m. Calibration contains eight fixed and 70 float solutions, whereas every evaluation solution is float. These observations expose poor transfer for this particular prescription and sequence; they do not identify its physical cause or rank positioning algorithms.

Serial dependence precludes an unqualified exchangeability-based coverage guarantee; counts have no iid binomial intervals. Combined processing can use later observations, so this is a retrospective split, not a leakage-free online experiment. The ten-epoch gap does not resolve these limitations or independently validate the reference. This diagnostic supplies neither independent issuer streams nor signed uncertainty. It motivates calibration validation without closing the protocol's multisource calibration requirement.
